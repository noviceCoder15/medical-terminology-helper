Anterior: toward the front of the body. In humans it means the same as ventral.

Posterior: toward the back of the body. In humans it means the same as dorsal.

Superior: above, or toward the head.

Inferior: below, or toward the feet.

Medial: toward the midline of the body.

Lateral: away from the midline, toward the side of the body.

Proximal: closer to the point of attachment or origin of a limb.

Distal: farther from the point of attachment or origin of a limb.

Sagittal plane: a vertical plane that divides the body into right and left parts.

Coronal plane: a vertical plane that divides the body into front and back parts.

Transverse plane: a horizontal plane that divides the body into upper and lower parts.

Homeostasis: the maintenance of a stable internal environment in the body.

Artery: a blood vessel that carries blood away from the heart.

Vein: a blood vessel that carries blood toward the heart.

Capillary: the smallest blood vessel, where gases, nutrients, and waste are exchanged between the blood and the tissues.

Atrium: an upper chamber of the heart that receives blood.

Ventricle: a lower chamber of the heart that pumps blood out of the heart.

Aorta: the largest artery in the body, which carries oxygenated blood from the left ventricle to the rest of the body.

Tachycardia: a heart rate faster than normal, generally above 100 beats per minute in an adult at rest.

Bradycardia: a heart rate slower than normal, generally below 60 beats per minute in an adult at rest.

Hypertension: abnormally high blood pressure.

Hypotension: abnormally low blood pressure.

Systole: the phase of the heartbeat in which the ventricles contract and pump blood out.

Diastole: the phase of the heartbeat in which the heart relaxes and fills with blood.

Myocardium: the muscular middle layer of the heart wall.

Pericardium: the double-layered sac that surrounds the heart.

Ischemia: an inadequate blood supply to a part of the body.

Hemoglobin: the iron-containing protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen.

Erythrocyte: a red blood cell.

Leukocyte: a white blood cell, which is part of the immune system.

Thrombocyte: a platelet, a cell fragment that helps the blood to clot.

Plasma: the liquid part of the blood.

Alveolus: a tiny air sac in the lung where gas exchange takes place.

Bronchus: one of the two main airways that lead from the trachea into the lungs.

Trachea: the windpipe, the tube that carries air from the larynx to the bronchi.

Diaphragm: the dome-shaped muscle beneath the lungs that is the main muscle of breathing.

Dyspnea: difficult or labored breathing, or shortness of breath.

Apnea: the temporary absence of breathing.

Hypoxia: a deficiency in the amount of oxygen reaching the tissues.

Pleura: the thin membrane that covers the lungs and lines the chest cavity.

Larynx: the voice box, which contains the vocal cords.

Pharynx: the throat, a passage shared by the respiratory and digestive systems.

Esophagus: the muscular tube that carries food from the throat to the stomach.

Peristalsis: waves of muscular contraction that move food along the digestive tract.

Duodenum: the first section of the small intestine, which receives bile and pancreatic juice.

Jejunum: the middle section of the small intestine.

Ileum: the final section of the small intestine, which joins the large intestine.

Colon: the main part of the large intestine, which absorbs water and forms feces.

Liver: the largest internal organ, which produces bile and processes nutrients.

Gallbladder: a small sac under the liver that stores bile.

Pancreas: a gland that produces digestive enzymes and the hormones insulin and glucagon.

Bile: a fluid made by the liver that helps to digest fats.

Dysphagia: difficulty in swallowing.

Neuron: a nerve cell that transmits electrical and chemical signals.

Axon: the long fiber of a neuron that carries impulses away from the cell body.

Dendrite: a branched extension of a neuron that receives signals from other cells.

Synapse: the junction between two neurons, or between a neuron and another cell, where a signal is passed on.

Cerebrum: the largest part of the brain, responsible for thought, memory, and voluntary movement.

Cerebellum: the part of the brain at the back of the head that coordinates movement and balance.

Meninges: the three membranes that cover the brain and spinal cord.

Cerebrospinal fluid: the clear fluid that surrounds and cushions the brain and spinal cord.

Paresthesia: an abnormal sensation, such as tingling or pins and needles.

Neuralgia: pain along the path of a nerve.

Aphasia: loss of the ability to produce or understand language, usually caused by damage to the brain.

Tendon: a tough band of tissue that connects muscle to bone.

Ligament: a tough band of tissue that connects one bone to another at a joint.

Cartilage: a firm, flexible connective tissue found in joints, the ears, and the nose.

Femur: the thigh bone, the longest and strongest bone in the body.

Humerus: the bone of the upper arm.

Tibia: the shinbone, the larger bone of the lower leg.

Scapula: the shoulder blade.

Clavicle: the collarbone.

Osteoporosis: a condition in which the bones become weak and brittle because of a loss of bone density.

Arthritis: inflammation of a joint.

Myalgia: muscle pain.

Hormone: a chemical messenger made by a gland and carried in the blood to act on target organs.

Insulin: a hormone made by the pancreas that lowers the level of glucose in the blood.

Thyroid: a gland in the neck that produces hormones that control metabolism.

Nephron: the functional unit of the kidney, which filters the blood and forms urine.

Ureter: a tube that carries urine from the kidney to the bladder.

Hematuria: the presence of blood in the urine.

Polyuria: the production of an abnormally large amount of urine.

Epidermis: the outer layer of the skin.

Dermis: the layer of skin beneath the epidermis, which contains blood vessels, nerves, and glands.

Edema: swelling caused by excess fluid in the body tissues.

Inflammation: the response of the body to injury or infection, marked by redness, heat, swelling, and pain.

Pathogen: a microorganism, such as a bacterium or a virus, that can cause disease.

Antibody: a protein made by the immune system that recognizes and binds a specific foreign substance.

Benign: not cancerous, and not spreading to other parts of the body.

Malignant: cancerous, and able to invade nearby tissue or spread to other parts of the body.

Anemia: a condition in which the blood has too few red blood cells or too little hemoglobin.

Hypoglycemia: an abnormally low level of glucose in the blood.

Hyperglycemia: an abnormally high level of glucose in the blood.

Diabetes mellitus: a disease in which the body does not make enough insulin or cannot use it properly, causing a high level of glucose in the blood.

Pneumonia: an infection that inflames the air sacs of one or both lungs.

Appendicitis: inflammation of the appendix.

Myocardial infarction: death of heart muscle caused by a blocked blood supply, commonly called a heart attack.

Stroke: a sudden loss of brain function caused by a blocked or ruptured blood vessel in the brain.

Anaphylaxis: a severe, life-threatening allergic reaction.

Syncope: a temporary loss of consciousness caused by a drop in blood flow to the brain, commonly called fainting.

Jaundice: a yellowing of the skin and the whites of the eyes, caused by too much bilirubin in the blood.

Cyanosis: a bluish color of the skin caused by a low level of oxygen in the blood.

Pyrexia: fever, or a body temperature above the normal range.

Hemostasis: the stopping of bleeding.

Hematoma: a collection of blood outside the blood vessels, as in a bruise.

The prefix hyper- means above, over, or excessive, as in hypertension.

The prefix hypo- means below, under, or deficient, as in hypoglycemia.

The prefix tachy- means fast, and the prefix brady- means slow, as in tachycardia and bradycardia.

The suffix -itis means inflammation, as in arthritis and appendicitis.

The suffix -ectomy means surgical removal, as in appendectomy.

The suffix -emia means a condition of the blood, as in anemia.

The suffix -pathy means disease, as in neuropathy.

The root cardi- refers to the heart, and cardiology is the study of the heart.

The root nephr- refers to the kidney, and nephritis is inflammation of the kidney.

The root hepat- refers to the liver, and hepatitis is inflammation of the liver.

The root gastr- refers to the stomach, and gastritis is inflammation of the lining of the stomach.

The root neur- refers to nerves, and neurology is the study of the nervous system.

The root derm- refers to the skin, and dermatology is the study of the skin.

The root osteo- refers to bone, and osteoarthritis is a disease of the joints in which the cartilage wears away.
